\paragraph{The problem: synaptic learning rules are hidden, but activity changes are not.}
A central question in neuroscience is whether biological neural networks optimise a loss function by adjusting synaptic weights---and if so, by what rule.  Decades of theoretical work have proposed candidate learning rules.  But synaptic weights cannot be measured at scale in behaving animals, so these proposals remain difficult to test directly.  Neural \emph{activities}, by contrast, can be recorded in large populations across learning.  This raises a concrete question: if the brain does perform gradient descent on its weights, what should we expect to see in the activities?  Here we show that the answer takes a surprisingly structured form: weight-space gradient descent induces kernel-filtered descent in activity space, and in diagonal-dominant regimes this reduces to approximately per-neuron activity descent.  The resulting prediction is directly testable with existing experimental techniques.

\paragraph{Setup and derivation.}
Consider a feedforward network with layers $\ell=1,\ldots,L$, weights $\vW$, and neural activities $A_i^{(\ell)}(\vW)$, trained to minimise a loss $\loss$.  Gradient descent on the weights embodies a simple principle: \emph{the more that strengthening a synapse would improve performance, the more that synapse is strengthened} (and vice versa---harmful synapses are weakened).  Formally, every weight is updated simultaneously:
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta W_\alpha = -\eta\,\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial W_\alpha}\,,
  \qquad \forall\;\alpha.
\end{equation}
%
What is the induced change in the activity of neuron $i$ in layer~$\ell$?  Because $A_i^{(\ell)}$ depends only on weights in layers $1$ through~$\ell$, writing $\vW_{1:\ell}$ for that block and Taylor-expanding around the pre-update weights $\vW_t$ gives
\[
  A_i^{(\ell)}(\vW_t + \Delta\vW)
  = A_i^{(\ell)}(\vW_t)
  + \nabla_{\!\vW_{1:\ell}} A_i^{(\ell)}(\vW_t)^{\!\top}\,\Delta\vW_{1:\ell}
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta\vW_{1:\ell}^{\!\top} H_i^{(\ell)}(\widetilde{\vW})\,\Delta\vW_{1:\ell},
\]
for some $\widetilde{\vW}$ between $\vW_t$ and $\vW_t+\Delta\vW$.  Substituting $\Delta W_\alpha = -\eta\,\partial\loss/\partial W_\alpha$ and dropping the $O(\eta^2)$ remainder gives
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
  \Delta A_i^{(\ell)}
  \;\approx\; -\eta \sum_{\alpha\in\text{layers}\,1..\ell} \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}\,\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial W_\alpha}\,,
\end{equation}
%
where the approximation is first-order in~$\eta$ (the remainder is $O(\eta^2)$, involving second derivatives of the activities with respect to the weights).  For piecewise-linear activations such as ReLU, if the step is small enough that no unit changes activation status, the network remains within a single smooth piecewise-polynomial region of weight space.  The first-order expression is then free of kink-crossing effects, but higher-order cross-layer terms generally remain when weights in multiple layers are updated simultaneously (e.g.\ contributions of the form $\Delta\vW^{(2)}\Delta\vW^{(1)}\vA^{(0)}$); these terms are $O(\eta^2)$.
%
In a sequential feedforward network (one without skip or residual connections), the effect of any weight in layers $1..\ell$ on the loss passes entirely through the activities of layer~$\ell$, so $\partial \loss/\partial W_\alpha = \sum_{k\in\text{layer}\,\ell} (\partial \loss/\partial A_k^{(\ell)})\,(\partial A_k^{(\ell)}/\partial W_\alpha)$.  Exchanging the sums yields
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
  \boxed{\;\Delta A_i^{(\ell)} \;\approx\; -\eta \sum_{k\in\text{layer}\,\ell} \Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}\;\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_k^{(\ell)}}\;,}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
  \Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}
  \;\coloneqq\; \sum_{\alpha\in\text{layers}\,1..\ell} \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}\,\frac{\partial A_k^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  \;=\; \grad A_i^{(\ell)} \cdot \grad A_k^{(\ell)}
\end{equation}
%
is a neural-tangent-kernel-style Gram matrix defined on the activities within each layer, and $\partial \loss/\partial A_k^{(\ell)}$ is the full backpropagated loss gradient---a sufficient statistic that compresses everything downstream layers contribute to the weight updates affecting layer~$\ell$.  In vector form, $\Delta\vA^{(\ell)} \approx -\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}\,\nabla_{\!\vA^{(\ell)}}\loss$: gradient descent in weight space induces \emph{kernel descent} within each layer of activity space.  The algebraic identity relating the Jacobian-weighted gradient to the kernel form is exact; the overall expression is a first-order approximation in~$\eta$ (see Eq.\,2).  It holds for any differentiable sequential feedforward network---i.e.\ one in which each layer receives input only from the immediately preceding layer---regardless of depth, width, or activation function.  Architectures with skip or residual connections require a modified treatment because weights can influence the loss through paths that bypass intermediate layers.

\paragraph{The self term drives each neuron toward its own loss gradient.}
The diagonal element $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}=\lVert\grad A_i^{(\ell)}\rVert^2$ collects contributions from \emph{all} weights that influence neuron $i$---both its own incoming weights and every weight in earlier layers that lies on a path to it.  Because $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\ge 0$---and is strictly positive for active units with nonzero upstream drive and at least one effective weight-to-activity path---the self term is aligned with $-\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$: each such neuron's activity moves down its own loss gradient, with an effective learning rate $\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ that grows with the depth and width of its input pathways.  Dead ReLU units have $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}=0$ and contribute no self-term drive.  In other words, weight-space gradient descent guarantees that every active neuron has a built-in tendency to change its activity in the loss-reducing direction.

\paragraph{Cross terms introduce interference from other neurons.}
The off-diagonal elements $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}=\grad A_i^{(\ell)}\cdot\grad A_k^{(\ell)}$ for $k\neq i$ couple neuron $i$ to every other neuron's loss gradient \emph{within the same layer}.  Their magnitude reflects similarity of weight-space sensitivities, often arising from shared upstream parameters: two neurons whose activities depend on overlapping upstream weight directions can have large $|\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}|$, whereas neurons supported by effectively orthogonal parameter directions have $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}\approx 0$.  These cross terms have no reason to be aligned with $-\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$ and constitute interference---the side-effect of the rest of the layer learning simultaneously.  Whether the self term or the cross terms dominate determines whether activity changes look like activity-space gradient descent or something messier.

\iffalse
\paragraph{Wide networks make the kernel diagonal-dominant.}
The cross terms vanish when $\grad A_i^{(\ell)} \cdot \grad A_k^{(\ell)} \approx 0$ for $i\neq k$---that is, when different neurons' activities depend on nearly orthogonal directions in weight space.  Several settings push the kernel toward this regime.

\emph{Width.}  Two neurons in the same layer share no incoming weights, so their activity gradients are independent random vectors in parameter space.  By concentration of measure, their inner product scales as $O(1/\sqrt{d})$ while the diagonal scales as $O(1)$, making $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ increasingly diagonal-dominant as width grows---the internal-activity analogue of the NTK becoming deterministic at infinite width.

\emph{Depth and weight sharing.}  Neurons in the same layer share all earlier-layer weights, which introduces a common component into their parameter gradients.  Each neuron's own incoming weights act as independent random projections that decorrelate this shared component in the wide limit.  Deeper networks have more shared parameters but also more such projections; in practice, the cross terms remain significant at finite width.

\emph{Learning dynamics.}  Gradient descent itself may reinforce diagonal dominance.  Training is known to decorrelate neural representations as the network learns to tile the input space efficiently.  To the extent that decorrelated activities imply decorrelated parameter gradients---since $\partial A_i^{(\ell)}/\partial W_\alpha$ depends on upstream activations feeding into $i$---learning should progressively shrink the off-diagonal entries of $\Phi^{(\ell)}$.  The link between activity and gradient decorrelation is heuristic and deserves formal treatment, but it suggests that trained networks may be closer to the diagonal regime than their initializations.

Taken together, these arguments imply that in sufficiently wide networks---and cortical circuits are very wide---the kernel $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is approximately diagonal, and activity changes closely track the negative activity-space loss gradient.

\paragraph{When is the kernel approximately diagonal?}
The cross terms are small when $\grad A_i^{(\ell)} \cdot \grad A_k^{(\ell)} \approx 0$ for $i\neq k$---that is, when different neurons' activities depend on nearly orthogonal directions in weight space.  Whether this occurs depends on the architecture, the parameterization, and the learned representation; it is not guaranteed by width alone.

\emph{Width.}  Increasing width gives each neuron more private incoming parameters, which can enlarge the self term $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$.  But neurons in the same layer also share all upstream parameters, so their activity gradients are generally not independent.  The off-diagonal terms therefore need not vanish simply by concentration of measure.  In some architectures and regimes, increasing width may still reduce the relative importance of the cross terms, but this is an empirical question rather than a generic theorem.

\emph{Depth and parameter sharing.}  Deeper layers inherit a larger shared upstream parameter set, which can strengthen correlations between neurons' activity gradients.  At the same time, each neuron also has its own incoming weights, which contribute a private component to $\grad A_i^{(\ell)}$.  The balance between these shared and private contributions determines whether $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is close to diagonal, and there is no reason to expect the answer to be universal across depths or architectures.

\emph{Learning dynamics.}  Training may also change this balance.  If learning decorrelates representations or routes different neurons through increasingly distinct effective pathways, the off-diagonal entries of $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ may shrink relative to the diagonal.  But this link is heuristic and should be treated as a hypothesis to be checked, not assumed.

Taken together, these considerations suggest treating diagonal dominance as a regime that may emerge in some networks, rather than as a general consequence of width.  When the diagonal terms dominate the off-diagonal terms, activity changes closely track the negative activity-space loss gradient; when they do not, the full kernel expression in Eq.\,3 remains the correct description.

\paragraph{The diagonal approximation: activity descent as a proxy for weight descent.}
Combining the kernel identity (Eq.\,3) with diagonal dominance yields a simple approximation:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diag}
  \Delta A_i^{(\ell)} \;\approx\; -\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\;\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}\,.
\end{equation}
%
Splitting the kernel update into self and cross terms,
\[
  \Delta A_i^{(\ell)}
  \;\approx\; -\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\,\frac{\partial\loss}{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}
  \;-\;\eta\sum_{k\neq i}\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}\,\frac{\partial\loss}{\partial A_k^{(\ell)}}\,,
\]
the operative condition for the diagonal approximation is that the gradient-weighted off-diagonal interference is small relative to the gradient-weighted self term,
\[
  \Bigl|\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\,\partial\loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}\Bigr|
  \;\gg\;
  \Bigl|\sum_{k\neq i}\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}\,\partial\loss/\partial A_k^{(\ell)}\Bigr|\,.
\]
Pure row-wise diagonal dominance of $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ ($\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\gg\sum_{k\neq i}|\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ik}|$) is a sufficient condition when within-layer gradient magnitudes $|\partial\loss/\partial A_k^{(\ell)}|$ are comparable across $k$; the gradient-weighted condition is the relevant one in general.  The intuitive meaning mirrors that of weight descent: just as weight descent says \emph{strengthen each synapse in proportion to how much it helps}, activity descent says \emph{increase each neuron's firing in proportion to how much that increase would help} (and decrease it when it hurts).
\iffalse
Equation~5 shows that one implies the other in wide networks---the per-neuron scaling $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ may differ across neurons, but every neuron's activity moves in the direction that locally reduces the loss.  This is the paper's central result: \emph{if a network performs gradient descent on its weights, and the network is wide enough, then its activity changes must approximately follow gradient descent in activity space.}  The approximation improves with width and, as our simulations show (Figs.\,1--2), is already accurate at moderate widths.
\fi
Equation~5 therefore identifies a useful approximation regime: when $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is sufficiently diagonal-dominant, each neuron's activity moves in the direction that locally reduces the loss, up to a neuron-specific scaling factor $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$.  The first-order kernel relation is always Eq.\,3; Eq.\,5 is a further empirical approximation whose quality depends on the network and training regime.

\paragraph{Recursive structure.}
The per-layer kernel inherits a recursion from the feedforward computation.  Writing $J_\ell = \partial\vA^{(\ell)}/\partial\vA^{(\ell-1)}$ for the inter-layer Jacobian and $D_\ell = \mathrm{diag}([\sigma'(z_i^{(\ell)})]^2\,\lVert\vA^{(\ell-1)}\rVert^2)$ for the diagonal kernel from layer~$\ell$'s own incoming weights:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-recursion}
  \Phi^{(\ell)} = D_\ell + J_\ell\,\Phi^{(\ell-1)}\,J_\ell^T\,,
  \qquad
  \Phi^{(1)} = D_1\,.
\end{equation}
%
$D_\ell$ is always diagonal---own-layer weights never couple same-layer neurons.  All off-diagonal structure comes from $J_\ell\,\Phi^{(\ell-1)}\,J_\ell^T$: earlier-layer weight updates propagated forward.
\iffalse
Width decorrelates $J_\ell$ at every stage, so diagonal dominance strengthens recursively.
\fi
When the inter-layer Jacobians decorrelate across neurons, this recursion can amplify diagonal structure from earlier layers; when shared upstream structure remains strong, substantial off-diagonal terms can persist.

\paragraph{A testable prediction for neuroscience.}
This result converts an untestable claim about synaptic learning rules into a testable prediction about observable activity changes.  Synaptic weights cannot be measured at scale in vivo, but neural activities can.
\iffalse
If a biological circuit performs \emph{any} form of gradient descent on its synaptic weights---even an approximate or noisy variant---and is sufficiently wide that $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is diagonal-dominant, then the observable activity changes must satisfy Eq.\,5.
\fi
If a biological circuit performs \emph{any} form of gradient descent on its synaptic weights---even an approximate or noisy variant---and operates in a regime where $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is approximately diagonal-dominant, then the observable activity changes must satisfy Eq.\,5.  The per-neuron scaling $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ is unknown and may vary across neurons, but the \emph{sign} of each active neuron's activity change is pinned: because $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\ge 0$ (and is strictly positive for active units), every such neuron must move in the direction that locally reduces the loss, i.e.\ $\operatorname{sign}(\Delta A_i^{(\ell)}) \approx \operatorname{sign}(-\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)})$.  Note that if $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ varies substantially across neurons, the population vector $\Delta\vA^{(\ell)}$ will not be parallel to $-\nabla_{\!\vA^{(\ell)}}\loss$ but rather to $-\mathrm{diag}(\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii})\,\nabla_{\!\vA^{(\ell)}}\loss$; the two directions coincide only when $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ is approximately uniform.  A concrete experimental protocol follows: record neural population activity across learning trials, estimate $\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$ from a task-fitted model, and check whether the measured $\Delta A_i^{(\ell)}$ covaries positively with $-\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$ across neurons.  Experimentally, $\Delta A_i^{(\ell)}$ should be estimated for matched task conditions or repeated stimuli across learning, so that the measured change is attributable to learning rather than to changes in the input distribution or behavioral state.  The unknown $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ absorbs per-neuron scaling, so the test checks the sign pattern and rank-order correlation, not the exact magnitudes.  A robust failure of this sign alignment would falsify the conjunction of assumptions that the fitted downstream model gives the correct activity-space loss gradient, that the circuit is following a gradient-like synaptic update for that loss, and that the relevant activity kernel is sufficiently diagonal-dominant.  Because this test relies on estimating $\partial\loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$ from a fitted downstream model, a negative result is ambiguous unless that model can be independently validated.

\paragraph{Caveat: activity descent is necessary but not sufficient for weight descent.}
The converse is weaker: observing that activities follow their loss gradient constrains the projection of $\Delta\vW$ onto the row space of the activity Jacobian $J_{\!A} = \partial \vA/\partial \vW$, but says nothing about the (typically vast) null space of weight changes that leave activities unchanged on the current input.  Many weight updates can induce the same observed $\Delta\vA$; the minimum-norm update for a specified activity change is given by the Jacobian pseudoinverse, $\Delta\vW_{\min} = J_{\!A}^{\!\top}(J_{\!A}J_{\!A}^{\!\top})^{+}\Delta\vA$, not generally by backpropagation.  Vanilla weight-gradient descent $\Delta\vW_{\rm GD} = -\eta\,J_{\!A}^{\!\top}\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss$ is one particular row-space update: it induces kernel descent $\Delta\vA \approx -\eta\,J_{\!A}J_{\!A}^{\!\top}\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss$, but the activity change alone does not identify that weight-level rule.
\iffalse
In short, weight descent implies activity descent (in wide networks), but activity descent does not uniquely identify the weight-level rule.
\fi
In short, weight descent implies the first-order kernel relation in Eq.\,3, and implies the simpler activity-descent form in Eq.\,5 only when $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ is close to diagonal; activity descent itself does not uniquely identify the weight-level rule.  This asymmetry is a feature, not a bug: it means the test can \emph{falsify} gradient-based learning, even though it cannot confirm a specific synaptic rule.

\section{He initialisation, depth, and the raw-vs-scaled correlation predictions}

This appendix asks how the empirical correlations between $\Delta\vA$ and the gradient signal depend on depth $D$ and hidden width $n$ under He (Kaiming) initialisation, $W^{(\ell)}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,2/n_{\ell-1})$.  Two correlations are reported in Figs.\,\ref{fig:depth-sweep}--\ref{fig:width-sweep-appendix}: the \emph{raw} correlation $r_{\rm raw}=r(\Delta\vA,\,-\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss)$ and the \emph{scaled} correlation $r_{\rm scaled}=r(\Delta\vA,\,-\Phi_{ii}\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss)$.  Under the diagonal approximation $\Delta A_i^{(\ell)}\approx-\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\,g_i^{(\ell)}$ the scaled signal is precisely $\Delta\vA/\eta$, so its leading-order prediction is $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$, with deviations attributable to off-diagonal kernel terms, $O(\eta^2)$ Taylor error, and ReLU-kink crossings.  The raw correlation is the one with non-trivial depth dependence: the per-neuron gain $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ acts as a layer-dependent rescaling of the target.

\paragraph{Three variance-preservation properties of He init.}
For a ReLU MLP at initialisation, in the wide-Gaussian limit:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item[(i)] \emph{Forward signal preserved}: $\mathbb{E}\lVert\vA^{(\ell)}\rVert^2/n_\ell$ is independent of $\ell$ (design property of He init for ReLU).
  \item[(ii)] \emph{Backward signal preserved}: $\mathbb{E}\lVert\partial \loss/\partial\vA^{(\ell)}\rVert^2/n_\ell$ is also independent of $\ell$, by the dual He calculation.
  \item[(iii)] \emph{Per-layer Jacobian norms preserved}: $\mathbb{E}\lVert J^{(\ell\leftarrow m)}_{i,:}\rVert^2 = O(1)$, independent of $\ell-m$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Leading-order calculation for the raw correlation.}
Let $J^{(\ell\leftarrow r)} = \partial\vA^{(\ell)}/\partial\vA^{(r)}$, with $J^{(\ell\leftarrow\ell)} = I$.  The contribution of weights in layer $r$ to $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ is
\[
  C_{r\to\ell,i}
  \;=\;
  \sum_{p\in\text{layer}\,r}
  \bigl(J^{(\ell\leftarrow r)}_{ip}\bigr)^2\,
  [\sigma_r'(z_p^{(r)})]^2\,
  \lVert\vA^{(r-1)}\rVert^2\,,
\]
and the total kernel diagonal is the sum over source layers,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-ii-sum}
  \Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\;=\;\sum_{r=1}^{\ell} C_{r\to\ell,i}\,.
\end{equation}
Under He init, properties (i)--(iii) make each $C_{r\to\ell,i}$ scale with the source-layer fan-in: $C_{1\to\ell,i}$ scales with the input dimension $d=\lVert\vA^{(0)}\rVert^2/O(1)$, while $C_{r\to\ell,i}$ for $r\ge 2$ scales with the hidden width $n$.  To leading order,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-ii-scaling}
  \Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\;\propto\;d + (\ell-1)n\;\propto\;\rho + \ell - 1,
  \qquad \rho\coloneqq d/n.
\end{equation}
The earlier $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}\propto\ell$ approximation is the special case $\rho=1$, i.e.\ $d=n$.  Because our simulations hold $d=16$ fixed and vary $n$, the input contribution matters at every width tested.

Let $c_\ell\coloneqq\rho+\ell-1$.  Treating $g_i\coloneqq\partial\loss/\partial A_i^{(\ell)}$ as i.i.d.\ with variance independent of $\ell$, and pooling neurons uniformly over hidden layers $\ell=1,\dots,D$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-derivation}
  r_{\rm raw}(D,\rho)
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{\mathbb{E}_\ell[c_\ell]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_\ell[c_\ell^2]}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\rho + (D-1)/2}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + \rho(D-1) + (D-1)(2D-1)/6}}.
\end{equation}
For $\rho=1$ this reduces to $\sqrt{3(D+1)/(2(2D+1))}\to\sqrt{3}/2$.  For fixed $d$ and $n\to\infty$ ($\rho\to 0$), the limit is depth-dependent: $r_{\rm raw}(D,0) = (D-1)/2\bigm/\sqrt{(D-1)(2D-1)/6}$, which gives $\sqrt{3/5}\approx 0.775$ at $D=3$ and tends to $\sqrt{3}/2$ as $D\to\infty$.  For the depth sweep with $\rho=d/n=0.5$, Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-derivation} sits between $\approx 0.89$ at $D=2$ and $\approx 0.87$ at $D=8$ -- nearly flat across this range because $\rho$ partially compensates the depth growth in $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$.

For the scaled correlation, the layer-dependent gain $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ is included in the comparison target, so gain heterogeneity no longer penalizes the correlation; the leading-order prediction is $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$ regardless of $\rho$ or $D$.

\paragraph{Exact first-order target: the full kernel signal.}
Suppress the layer index and write the exact first-order prediction as
\[
  \vy \;\coloneqq\; \eta^{-1}\Delta\vA \;\approx\; \Phi\,\vh,
  \qquad \vh \;\coloneqq\; -\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss.
\]
The natural baseline target is $\Phi\vh$ itself: $r_{\rm full}\coloneqq r(\vy,\Phi\vh)\approx 1$ at first order in $\eta$, modulo $O(\eta^2)$ Taylor error and ReLU kink crossings.  The raw and scaled correlations are lower-dimensional summaries of how well the cheaper targets $\vh$ and $D\vh$ approximate the full kernel signal, where $D\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(\Phi)$ and $R\coloneqq\Phi-D$:
\[
  \vy \;=\; \Phi\vh \;=\; \underbrace{D\vh}_{\vs} \;+\; \underbrace{R\vh}_{\vu}.
\]
With $\Phi$ known per snapshot, all three correlations are directly computable; no diagonal-dominance approximation is needed.

\paragraph{Exact form of the scaled correlation.}
Working with centered inner products $\langle\vu,\vv\rangle_c=(P\vu)^{\!\top}(P\vv)$, $P=I-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\!\top}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:snr}
  r_{\rm scaled}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\lVert\vs\rVert^2 + \langle\vs,\vu\rangle}
       {\sqrt{\bigl(\lVert\vs\rVert^2 + 2\langle\vs,\vu\rangle + \lVert\vu\rVert^2\bigr)\,\lVert\vs\rVert^2}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{1+\kappa}{\sqrt{1+2\kappa+\Gamma^{-1}}}\,,
\end{equation}
with $\Gamma\coloneqq\lVert\vs\rVert^2/\lVert\vu\rVert^2$ and $\kappa\coloneqq\langle\vs,\vu\rangle/\lVert\vs\rVert^2$.  No diagonal-dominance assumption is needed; the only approximation is in dropping $\kappa$, giving the clean SNR form $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1/\sqrt{1+\Gamma^{-1}}$ when the off-diagonal interference $\vu=R\vh$ is approximately uncorrelated with the diagonal signal $\vs=D\vh$.

\paragraph{Trace formulas under isotropic gradients.}
If $\vh$ is approximately isotropic in activity space ($\mathbb{E}[\vh\vh^{\!\top}]\propto I$) and independent of $\Phi$, then taking expectations over $\vh$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rraw-trace}
  r_{\rm raw}
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{\mathrm{tr}(\Phi)}{\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{CV}(\lambda)^2}}\,,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\Phi$ and $\mathrm{CV}(\lambda)$ their coefficient of variation, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rscaled-trace}
  r_{\rm scaled}
  \;\approx\;
  \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(D^2)}{\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{i\neq k}\Phi_{ik}^2\,\big/\,\sum_i\Phi_{ii}^2}}\,.
\end{equation}
The first is a \emph{spectral coefficient-of-variation} statement: $r_{\rm raw}$ is high when $\Phi$ has a narrow spectrum and falls when $\Phi$ amplifies some activity modes much more than others.  The second is a \emph{Frobenius diagonal-mass} statement: $r_{\rm scaled}$ is high when the squared diagonal entries dominate the squared off-diagonal entries.  Because $\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)=\mathrm{tr}(D^2)+\mathrm{tr}(R^2)$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\Phi)=\mathrm{tr}(D)$, the two combine multiplicatively:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-raw-snr}
  r_{\rm raw}
  \;\approx\;
  \underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(D)}{\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{tr}(D^2)}}}_{\text{gain-heterogeneity factor}}
  \;\cdot\;
  \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(D^2)}{\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)}}}_{=\;\widehat r_{\rm scaled}}\,.
\end{equation}
The first factor is one when $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$ is uniform; the second is the off-diagonal SNR penalty.  In particular, in the isotropic-$\vh$ approximation $r_{\rm raw}\le r_{\rm scaled}$, with equality only when $\Phi$ has a uniform diagonal.

If instead $\mathbb{E}[\vh\vh^{\!\top}]=C_h$ is anisotropic, the trace formulas generalise to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace-Ch}
  r_{\rm raw}
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{\mathrm{tr}(\Phi C_h)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(C_h)\,\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2 C_h)}}\,,
  \qquad
  r_{\rm scaled}
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{\mathrm{tr}(D\Phi C_h)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(D^2 C_h)\,\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2 C_h)}}\,.
\end{equation}
These make explicit that the correlations depend on where $\vh$ lives relative to the eigenspaces of $\Phi$: if $\vh$ aligns with one eigenvector of $\Phi$, then $\Phi\vh\propto\vh$ and $r_{\rm raw}\approx 1$ even when $\Phi$ is far from diagonal.

\paragraph{Pooled across layers.}
When neurons from all hidden layers are concatenated, the relevant kernel is block-diagonal $\Phi_{\rm pool}=\mathrm{blockdiag}(\Phi^{(1)},\dots,\Phi^{(D)})$.  If $\mathbb{E}[\vh^{(\ell)}\vh^{(\ell)\top}]=S_\ell I$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pooled}
  r_{\rm raw}
  \approx
  \frac{\sum_\ell S_\ell\,\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{(\ell)})}
       {\sqrt{\bigl(\sum_\ell S_\ell n_\ell\bigr)\bigl(\sum_\ell S_\ell\,\mathrm{tr}[(\Phi^{(\ell)})^2]\bigr)}}\,,
  \quad
  r_{\rm scaled}
  \approx
  \sqrt{\frac{\sum_\ell S_\ell\,\mathrm{tr}[(D^{(\ell)})^2]}{\sum_\ell S_\ell\,\mathrm{tr}[(\Phi^{(\ell)})^2]}}\,.
\end{equation}
Three effects separate cleanly: $S_\ell$ captures gradient-magnitude drift across layers; $\mathrm{tr}[(D^{(\ell)})^2]$ is the per-layer diagonal signal mass; $\mathrm{tr}[(\Phi^{(\ell)})^2]-\mathrm{tr}[(D^{(\ell)})^2]$ is the per-layer off-diagonal interference mass.  The leading-order calculation of Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-derivation} corresponds to $D^{(\ell)}=\mu_\ell I$, $R^{(\ell)}=0$, and $S_\ell$ constant, which collapses Eq.\,\ref{eq:pooled} to $r_{\rm raw}\approx \mathbb{E}_\ell[\mu_\ell]/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_\ell[\mu_\ell^2]}$ and $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$.

\paragraph{Random-matrix baselines for $\Phi$.}
Eqs.\,\ref{eq:rraw-trace}--\ref{eq:rscaled-trace} hold for any $\Phi$; what changes between models is the spectrum.  Under standard parameterisations $\Phi=J_A J_A^{\!\top}$ becomes deterministic in the infinite-width limit (the NTK), but at finite width it is a random Gram matrix whose spectrum carries the off-diagonal mass.

\emph{Wishart null.}  If $J_A=(\tau/\sqrt{p})X$ with $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ having i.i.d.\ mean-zero unit-variance entries, then $\Phi$ is Wishart with aspect ratio $q=n/p$; its first two moments are $m_1=\tau^2$, $m_2=\tau^4(1+q)$, and the diagonal concentrates to $\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\sum_i\Phi_{ii}^2\approx\tau^4$.  Eqs.\,\ref{eq:rraw-trace}--\ref{eq:rscaled-trace} then give
\[
  r_{\rm raw}\;\approx\;r_{\rm scaled}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q}}\,.
\]
This is a useful sanity check: if the effective parameter dimension $p$ is much larger than the number of neurons $n$, both correlations should be close to one.  Empirically $r_{\rm scaled}\ll 1$ therefore indicates that the rows of $J_A$ are not independent isotropic vectors in parameter space -- they share structured upstream pathways through the recursion $\Phi^{(\ell)}=D_\ell+J_\ell\Phi^{(\ell-1)}J_\ell^{\!\top}$.

\emph{Recursive Wishart model.}  A more realistic null splits $\Phi^{(\ell)}$ into a private own-layer contribution and shared upstream contributions:
\[
  \Phi^{(\ell)} \;\approx\; a_{\ell,0}\,I \;+\; \sum_{r<\ell} a_{\ell,r}\,W_{\ell,r}\,,
\]
where each $W_{\ell,r}$ is approximately Wishart with aspect ratio $q_{\ell,r}$.  Then $m_{1,\ell}\approx a_{\ell,0}+\sum_{r<\ell}a_{\ell,r}$ and $m_{2,\ell}\approx m_{1,\ell}^2+\sum_{r<\ell}a_{\ell,r}^2 q_{\ell,r}$, so the off-diagonal Frobenius mass is $\sum_{r<\ell}a_{\ell,r}^2 q_{\ell,r}$ -- the spectral broadening grows with the number of propagated upstream contributions.  Large private own-layer mass keeps $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$; large shared upstream mass pushes it down.  Depth increases the number and importance of propagated terms, so $r_{\rm scaled}$ should fall with $D$.  Width helps only if it raises the private diagonal mass faster than the shared off-diagonal mass.  He initialisation controls $m_{1,\ell}$ (and hence the leading-order prediction Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-derivation}) but does not by itself force a narrow spectrum; away from dynamical isometry, the singular-value distribution of propagated Jacobians broadens with depth, inflating $m_{2,\ell}$ relative to $m_{1,\ell}^2$.

\paragraph{Diagnostic measurements.}
Per snapshot and layer, four quantities decompose the residual gap cleanly:
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item $r_{\rm full} = r(\Delta\vA,\Phi\vh)$ -- should be $\approx 1$ when the Taylor step is small and no ReLU kink crossings dominate;
  \item $r_{\rm scaled} = r(\Delta\vA, D\vh)$ -- measured scaled correlation;
  \item $\widehat r_{\rm scaled} = \sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(D^2)/\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)}$ -- trace prediction under isotropic $\vh$ (Eq.\,\ref{eq:rscaled-trace});
  \item $\widehat r_{\rm raw} = \mathrm{tr}(\Phi)/\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^2)}$ -- trace prediction under isotropic $\vh$ (Eq.\,\ref{eq:rraw-trace}).
\end{itemize}
Agreement between measured and hatted predictions validates the isotropic-$\vh$ approximation; disagreement points to non-trivial alignment of $\vh$ with eigenspaces of $\Phi$, in which case the covariance-weighted forms in Eq.\,\ref{eq:trace-Ch} are the appropriate next step.

\paragraph{Where the prediction holds and where it breaks.}
Fig.\,\ref{fig:depth-sweep} sweeps depth from 2 to 8 at fixed width $n=32$ ($\rho=0.5$).  Three things are visible:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item \emph{The raw prediction is close at small $D$ and diverges at large $D$.}  At $D=2$ the $\rho=0.5$ prediction is $\approx 0.89$ vs.\ empirical $\approx 0.86$ (gap $\approx 0.03$); at $D=8$ the prediction is still $\approx 0.87$ but the empirical raw curve has dropped to $\approx 0.63$ (gap $\approx 0.24$).  The leading-order calculation captures the small-depth value well but does not capture the depth-dependent loss in correlation.
  \item \emph{The scaled correlation also undershoots its prediction, more sharply with depth.}  $r_{\rm scaled}$ runs from $\approx 0.95$ at $D=2$ down to $\approx 0.73$ at $D=8$, even though the leading-order prediction is $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$ at every depth.
  \item \emph{Init and trained behave nearly identically.}  The left and right panels are within noise of each other, so the depth-dependence is a property of the initialisation regime itself, not a consequence of training.
\end{enumerate}
The trace decomposition (Eqs.\,\ref{eq:rraw-trace}--\ref{eq:pooled}) places both gaps in a unified framework.  The drop in $r_{\rm scaled}$ with depth reflects off-diagonal Frobenius mass growing relative to diagonal: $\sum_{i\neq k}\Phi^{(\ell)2}_{ik}/\sum_i\Phi^{(\ell)2}_{ii}$ rises through the propagated Wishart-like contributions $W_{\ell,r}$ generated by the recursion $\Phi^{(\ell)}=D_\ell + J_\ell\Phi^{(\ell-1)}J_\ell^{\!\top}$, exactly as the recursive Wishart model predicts.  This is the only first-order source of $r_{\rm scaled}<1$.  The further gap in $r_{\rm raw}$ comes from the gain-heterogeneity factor $\mathrm{tr}(D)/\sqrt{n\,\mathrm{tr}(D^2)}$ in Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-raw-snr}: layers contribute very different mean diagonals (Eq.\,\ref{eq:phi-ii-scaling}) and within each layer there is finite-width scatter in $\Phi^{(\ell)}_{ii}$, both of which inflate $\mathrm{tr}(D^2)$ relative to $\mathrm{tr}(D)^2/n$.  Any residual mismatch between the measured correlations and the isotropic-$\vh$ trace predictions $\widehat r_{\rm raw},\widehat r_{\rm scaled}$ indicates non-trivial alignment of the backpropagated $\vh$ with eigenspaces of $\Phi$, in which case the covariance-weighted forms (Eq.\,\ref{eq:trace-Ch}) are the right next step.

\paragraph{Implication.}
Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-derivation} is the leading-order wide-network prediction for the raw correlation, parameterised by $\rho=d/n$ and $D$.  The simpler $\sqrt{3(D+1)/(2(2D+1))}\to\sqrt{3}/2$ formula is its $\rho=1$ (equal input and hidden width) special case; it should not be applied at $\rho\ne 1$.  The scaled-gradient comparison has the cleaner leading-order prediction $r_{\rm scaled}\approx 1$, independent of $\rho$ and $D$; its deviation isolates the off-diagonal kernel contribution.  The interactive demo allows the reader to verify both panels of Fig.\,\ref{fig:depth-sweep} live.

\paragraph{Width sweep at fixed depth.}
The complementary experiment holds depth at $D=3$ and varies width $n\in\{4,\dots,128\}$, so $\rho=d/n=16/n$ varies from $4$ down to $1/8$.  Eq.\,\ref{eq:r-derivation} at $D=3$ becomes
\[
  r_{\rm raw}(3,\rho) = \frac{\rho+1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+2\rho+5/3}},
\]
which falls from $\approx 0.99$ at $n=4$ to $\sqrt{3/5}\approx 0.775$ as $n\to\infty$ at fixed $d$.  This is the appropriate baseline for the width sweep -- the often-quoted $\sqrt{6/7}\approx 0.926$ would only apply at $d=n$, which is realised only at $n=16$ in this sweep.

\section{Annotated layer-2 decomposition}

To make the diagonal/off-diagonal split concrete, consider a standard layer-2 block (omitting biases for simplicity):
\[
  z_i^{(2)} = \sum_m W_{im}^{(2)} A_m^{(1)},
  \qquad
  A_i^{(2)} = \sigma_2\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr).
\]
The first-order activity update is
\[
  \Delta A_i^{(2)}
  \approx
  -\eta \sum_j \Phi_{ij}^{(2)} \frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_j^{(2)}}.
\]
The point of this appendix is to show explicitly where the self term $\Phi_{ii}^{(2)}$ and the cross terms $\Phi_{ij}^{(2)}$ for $j\neq i$ come from.

\paragraph{Own-layer weights contribute only to the diagonal.}
For a weight $W_{pm}^{(2)}$ feeding directly into layer~2,
\[
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(2)}}
  =
  \delta_{ip}\,\sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr)\,A_m^{(1)}.
\]
Therefore the layer-2 contribution to the kernel is
\[
  \sum_{p,m}
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(2)}}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(2)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(2)}}
  =
  \delta_{ij}\,[\sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr)]^2\,\lVert \vA^{(1)} \rVert^2
  =
  \delta_{ij} D_{2,ii}.
\]
This term is diagonal because two different neurons in the same layer do not share their own incoming weights.

\paragraph{Earlier-layer weights are propagated forward by the Jacobian.}
Now take a weight $W_\alpha$ in layer~1.  By the chain rule,
\[
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  =
  \sum_m
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial A_m^{(1)}}
  \frac{\partial A_m^{(1)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  =
  \sum_m J_{2,im}
  \frac{\partial A_m^{(1)}}{\partial W_\alpha},
  \qquad
  J_{2,im} = \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial A_m^{(1)}}.
\]
Summing over all layer-1 weights gives
\[
  \sum_{\alpha\in\text{layer }1}
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(2)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(2)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  =
  \sum_{m,n} J_{2,im}\,\Phi_{mn}^{(1)}\,J_{2,jn}.
\]
Because layer~1 has no earlier shared weights, $\Phi^{(1)} = D_1$ is diagonal, so this reduces to
\[
  \sum_{m,n} J_{2,im}\,\Phi_{mn}^{(1)}\,J_{2,jn}
  =
  \sum_m J_{2,im}\,D_{1,mm}\,J_{2,jm}.
\]
Thus the full layer-2 kernel is
\[
  \Phi_{ij}^{(2)}
  =
  \delta_{ij} D_{2,ii}
  +
  \sum_m J_{2,im}\,D_{1,mm}\,J_{2,jm}.
\]
If one writes the Jacobian explicitly,
\[
  J_{2,im} = \sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr) W_{im}^{(2)},
\]
so
\[
  \Phi_{ij}^{(2)}
  =
  \delta_{ij} [\sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr)]^2 \lVert \vA^{(1)} \rVert^2
  +
  \sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr)\,\sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_j^{(2)}\bigr)
  \sum_m W_{im}^{(2)} D_{1,mm} W_{jm}^{(2)}.
\]

\paragraph{Self and cross terms in the activity update.}
Substituting the layer-2 kernel into the first-order update and separating the $j=i$ and $j\neq i$ pieces gives
\[
  \Delta A_i^{(2)}
  \approx
  -\eta \,\Phi_{ii}^{(2)}\,\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_i^{(2)}}
  -
  \eta \sum_{j\neq i} \Phi_{ij}^{(2)}\,\frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_j^{(2)}}.
\]
Using the expression above,
\[
  \Phi_{ii}^{(2)}
  =
  D_{2,ii}
  +
  \sum_m J_{2,im}^2 D_{1,mm},
\]
while for $j\neq i$,
\[
  \Phi_{ij}^{(2)}
  =
  \sum_m J_{2,im} D_{1,mm} J_{2,jm}.
\]
Hence
\[
  \Delta A_i^{(2)}
  \approx
  -\eta \Bigl(D_{2,ii} + \sum_m J_{2,im}^2 D_{1,mm}\Bigr) \frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_i^{(2)}}
  -
  \eta \sum_{j\neq i}
  \Bigl(\sum_m J_{2,im} D_{1,mm} J_{2,jm}\Bigr)
  \frac{\partial \loss}{\partial A_j^{(2)}}.
\]
The first bracket is the self term: it is always aligned with $-\partial \loss/\partial A_i^{(2)}$.  The second sum is the interference term: it mixes neuron $i$ with the gradients of other neurons in the same layer through shared earlier-layer parameters.

\paragraph{Relation to the temporary $x_0/x_1$ notation.}
If one writes $x_0 = \vA^{(0)}$ and $x_1 = \vA^{(1)}$, then
\[
  D_{1,mm} = [\sigma_1'\!\bigl(z_m^{(1)}\bigr)]^2 \lVert x_0 \rVert^2,
  \qquad
  D_{2,ii} = [\sigma_2'\!\bigl(z_i^{(2)}\bigr)]^2 \lVert x_1 \rVert^2.
\]
So the appearance of both $x_0$ and $x_1$ in scratch work is meaningful, not a typo: they are simply the activities entering layers~1 and~2.  In the main text, $\vA^{(0)}$ and $\vA^{(1)}$ are cleaner and more consistent with the rest of the notation.

\section{General recursion for deeper networks and nonlinearities}

For an arbitrary depth-$L$ feedforward network with pointwise nonlinearities,
\[
  z_i^{(\ell)} = \sum_m W_{im}^{(\ell)} A_m^{(\ell-1)},
  \qquad
  A_i^{(\ell)} = \sigma_\ell\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr),
  \qquad \ell = 1,\dots,L,
\]
again omitting biases for simplicity.  The first-order kernel relation from the main text still holds:
\[
  \Delta \vA^{(\ell)} \approx -\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}\,\nabla_{\!\vA^{(\ell)}} \loss,
  \qquad
  \Phi_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \sum_\alpha
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}.
\]
To obtain a recursion, split the weight sum into layer $\ell$ itself and layers $1,\dots,\ell-1$.

\paragraph{Own-layer contribution.}
For a weight $W_{pm}^{(\ell)}$ in layer~$\ell$,
\[
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(\ell)}}
  =
  \delta_{ip}\,\sigma_\ell'\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr)\,A_m^{(\ell-1)}.
\]
Therefore the own-layer contribution is diagonal:
\[
  \sum_{p,m}
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(\ell)}}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_{pm}^{(\ell)}}
  =
  \delta_{ij}[\sigma_\ell'\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr)]^2 \lVert \vA^{(\ell-1)} \rVert^2
  =
  \delta_{ij} D_{\ell,ii}.
\]

\paragraph{Earlier-layer contribution.}
Define the inter-layer Jacobian
\[
  J_{\ell,im} = \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial A_m^{(\ell-1)}}
  =
  \sigma_\ell'\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr) W_{im}^{(\ell)}.
\]
Then for any weight $W_\alpha$ in layers $1,\dots,\ell-1$,
\[
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  =
  \sum_m J_{\ell,im}\,\frac{\partial A_m^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial W_\alpha}.
\]
Summing over earlier-layer weights gives
\[
  \sum_{\alpha\in\text{layers }1..\ell-1}
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  =
  \sum_{m,n} J_{\ell,im}\,\Phi_{mn}^{(\ell-1)}\,J_{\ell,jn}.
\]
Combining both pieces yields the recursion
\[
  \Phi^{(\ell)} = D_\ell + J_\ell \,\Phi^{(\ell-1)}\, J_\ell^T,
  \qquad
  \Phi^{(1)} = D_1.
\]
This is exactly Eq.~\eqref{eq:phi-recursion} in expanded form.

\paragraph{Unrolling the recursion.}
Repeated substitution shows that every earlier layer contributes a positive-semidefinite term transported forward by the intermediate Jacobians:
\[
  \Phi^{(\ell)}
  =
  D_\ell
  + J_\ell D_{\ell-1} J_\ell^T
  + J_\ell J_{\ell-1} D_{\ell-2} J_{\ell-1}^T J_\ell^T
  + \cdots
  + J_\ell J_{\ell-1} \cdots J_2\, D_1\, J_2^T \cdots J_{\ell-1}^T J_\ell^T.
\]
This makes the structure transparent: $D_r$ captures the direct effect of layer-$r$ weights on layer-$r$ activities, and the surrounding Jacobians propagate that contribution forward to layer~$\ell$.

\paragraph{Role of nonlinearities.}
Nothing in the kernel definition or recursion requires linear hidden units.  For smooth nonlinearities such as sigmoid, tanh, or GELU, the algebraic derivation above holds with the corresponding $\sigma_\ell'$; the overall $\Delta\vA\approx-\eta\Phi\nabla_{\!\vA}\loss$ remains a first-order approximation in~$\eta$.  For piecewise-linear nonlinearities such as ReLU or leaky ReLU, the same formulas hold almost everywhere; at kink points one may use a subgradient.  In particular, if a ReLU unit is inactive on the sampled input, then $\sigma_\ell'\!\bigl(z_i^{(\ell)}\bigr)=0$, so its row of $J_\ell$ and its diagonal entry in $D_\ell$ vanish.  This is the appendix-level version of why dead ReLU units contribute nothing to the kernel on that input.

\paragraph{More general feedforward modules.}
The recursion above assumes an affine layer followed by a pointwise nonlinearity.  The kernel definition
\[
  \Phi_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \sum_\alpha
  \frac{\partial A_i^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
  \frac{\partial A_j^{(\ell)}}{\partial W_\alpha}
\]
and the first-order relation $\Delta\vA^{(\ell)}\approx-\eta\,\Phi^{(\ell)}\nabla_{\!\vA^{(\ell)}}\loss$ still hold for any differentiable sequential feedforward architecture (i.e.\ one without skip connections).  For other modules the same logic applies: there is an own-module contribution from parameters in layer~$\ell$ and a propagated earlier-layer contribution obtained by sandwiching $\Phi^{(\ell-1)}$ between the appropriate Jacobians.  Only the explicit forms of $D_\ell$ and $J_\ell$ change.
